# Singing Strip / Singing Ruler

**Archival text edition recovered from `Singing_Ruler_Microbit_Executive_Summary.pdf`.** The original formatted PDF remains preserved in the owner’s Library. This edition keeps the intellectual and experimental content searchable in GitHub.

## A low-cost, classroom-ready cantilever vibration lab using a Micro:bit v2 or M5StickC-class IMU controller

**Executive thesis:** a simple flat metal strip, clamped as a cantilever and instrumented with an inexpensive motion-sensing microcontroller, becomes a serious physics, data, coding, and mathematics laboratory. This is new and different because the experiment starts as an ordinary object but opens directly into real time-series measurement, damping, frequency, statistics, and differential-equation thinking.

> Button, candle, ruler: three simple objects that reveal time, energy, and motion.

Prepared for: Randall Young / UMA Motion  
Focus: Micro:bit v2 Singing Ruler / Singing Strip

## 1. Executive Summary

The proposed Micro:bit v2 Singing Ruler experiment converts a short metal strip into a measured cantilever oscillator. A student clamps one end of the strip, attaches a Micro:bit v2 near the free end, deflects the strip gently, releases it, and records acceleration over time. The resulting signal shows vibration, damping, frequency, amplitude decay, and repeatability. The physical setup is simple, but the intellectual reach is unusually broad.

The breakthrough is not merely that the Micro:bit can sense vibration. The breakthrough is that a low-cost classroom object becomes a complete pathway from visible motion to mathematical structure. The same event can be approached at many levels: observation, graphing, peak counting, period calculation, exponential decay, Fourier analysis, and eventually ordinary differential equations.

This makes the Singing Strip an especially strong UMA-style laboratory because it follows the same durable chain used across the broader work: **phenomenon -> sensor -> controller -> link -> dashboard -> record -> analysis -> interpretation.**

## 2. Why This Is New and Different

| Element | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ordinary object | The core apparatus is not a lab instrument. It is a metal strip, clamp, rubber bands, and a small educational microcontroller. |
| Real signal | The output is not a canned simulation. It is live acceleration data generated by a physical oscillator. |
| Mathematical soul | The motion naturally reveals period, frequency, damping, exponential envelopes, and eventually ODE models. |
| Low-cost bridge | The Micro:bit v2 version makes the experiment accessible to classrooms that cannot justify specialized motion probes. |
| Scalable curriculum | The same hardware can support MakeCode, MicroPython, USB serial, browser dashboards, Python analysis, and advanced modeling. |

The difference from many school vibration demos is that this is not only visual and qualitative. It is quantitative from the first run. The student sees the strip vibrate, watches the data stream, then calculates features of the motion from the recorded signal. That makes it a bridge between shop-built apparatus and modern computational science.

## 3. Recommended Hardware Specification

For a sturdy but responsive classroom version, the best starting geometry is a short stainless-steel flat strip. The target is not maximum stiffness; it is clean, repeatable vibration with enough movement for the onboard accelerometer to measure clearly.

| Item | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Preferred strip | 304 stainless steel flat bar |
| Recommended size | 250 mm long × 20 mm wide × 1 mm thick |
| In inches | About 10 in long × 0.8 in wide × 0.04 in thick |
| Clamp region | Clamp about 50 mm; leave about 200 mm free |
| Controller | Micro:bit v2 for classroom accessibility; M5StickC Plus 2 for the more advanced compact instrument version |
| Attachment | Rubber bands, Velcro straps, or light zip ties with a small foam pad to prevent slipping |

A pre-cut 250 mm stainless flat bar avoids the extra classroom burden of hacksaw cutting and gives repeatable test specimens.

Thickness selection is important. A 1 mm strip is the safest first choice because it should vibrate visibly even with the Micro:bit attached. A 2 mm strip may be useful for comparison or for a more rugged version, but 3 mm and 4 mm pieces are likely to be too stiff for a short 250 mm classroom cantilever unless the end mass is increased or the free length is extended.

## 4. Curriculum Position: The Breakthrough Triad

The Singing Strip belongs with two other recent breakthrough labs: the 1-Bit Reaction Game and the 2-Wire Candle Lab. Together they form a compact, powerful triad: time, energy, and motion.

| Lab | Role |
|---|---|
| 1-Bit Reaction Game | Measures human response time. It teaches event timing, one-bit input/output, trial data, mean, standard deviation, and histograms. |
| 2-Wire Candle Lab | Measures heat, mass loss, temperature rise, and energy transfer. It makes a flame into a quantified physical system. |
| Singing Strip | Measures oscillation and damping. It turns motion into graphs, frequencies, envelopes, and mathematical models. |

The triad is educationally unusual because each lab begins with a simple object — button, candle, ruler — and ends with a genuine data-analysis pathway.

## 5. Instructional Payoff

The Singing Strip can be introduced gently in MakeCode as a motion-sensing activity, then expanded into MicroPython or browser-based data capture. Students do not need to begin with abstract equations. They begin with a visible oscillation, capture data, identify peaks, calculate period, and then ask why the amplitude decreases. The mathematics enters as an explanation of something they have already seen.

This is the central instructional advantage: the experiment removes the artificial wall between hands-on science and mathematical modeling. The graph is not decoration; it is the measured behavior of the object in front of the student.

## 6. Recommended First Protocol

- Clamp 50 mm of the 250 mm stainless strip firmly to a table, leaving about 200 mm free.
- Attach the Micro:bit v2 near the free end using rubber bands or Velcro. Keep it aligned and secure.
- Connect by USB for the first version so data capture is simple and reliable.
- Start a timed accelerometer sampling loop.
- Pull the strip downward gently by a repeatable amount and release cleanly.
- Record acceleration versus time for 5 to 10 seconds.
- Plot the data, identify peaks, estimate period and frequency, then compare peak heights to discuss damping.

## 7. Core Claim

The Micro:bit Singing Strip is a new kind of low-cost physics laboratory: not a toy, not a simulation, and not an expensive probeware kit. It is a physical object with a measurable dynamic signature. It can be built cheaply, repeated across classrooms, and scaled from elementary observation to serious mathematical analysis.

Its importance is that it demonstrates the UMA principle in a particularly pure form: **make the invisible measurable, then make the measurement teach.**

> Reaction measures the person. Candle measures the world. Ruler measures motion. Together they create a compact, original, dashboard-ready curriculum for time, energy, and dynamics.
